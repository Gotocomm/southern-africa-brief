## Script
**[OPEN]**
Good morning. This is The Southern Africa Brief for Thursday, the 26th of November.
Today: a warning that progress for South Africa's children is stalling, delayed allowances for college students, a call for state companies to train more young people, and a new report on the state of our public schools.

**[STORY 1]**
The Children's Institute at the University of Cape Town launched the South African Child Gauge 2026 on Tuesday. Its message is that progress for children is stalling. The report says about 68 percent of children live below the upper-bound poverty line. 37 percent live below the food poverty line. 28 percent of children under five are stunted. And only 54 percent of children in early learning programmes are developmentally on track. One finding stands out. The number of babies receiving the Child Support Grant fell from 658 thousand in March 2020 to 366 thousand, 108 in March 2026. That is a drop of 44 percent.
Why it matters: the first thousand days of a child's life shape everything that follows. The report's editors say fiscal austerity has started to erode progress. The grant is 580 rand a month, still below the food poverty line of 868 rand per person.
What to do: if you know a new parent, encourage them to register the baby's birth as early as possible. Then apply for the Child Support Grant at the South African Social Security Agency. Community health workers, early childhood centres and churches can check that the families they serve have done both.

**[STORY 2]**
Students at technical and vocational education and training colleges, known as TVET colleges, are still waiting for their final 2026 allowances. The National Student Financial Aid Scheme, known as NSFAS, first set the payments for the 17th of November, then moved them to the 19th. They have now been delayed again, with no new date. NSFAS says the budget line for TVET allowances had run out. Its board has approved moving money to cover the payments. The Department of Higher Education and Training and the Minister must still approve that change. Deputy Minister Yusuf Cassim said students already approved for funding should not be left without their final allowances.
Why it matters: students rely on this funding for accommodation, tuition and other costs. A late payment at the end of the year can leave a student unable to pay rent or buy food.
What to do: students, wait for the revised date from NSFAS itself, through its official channels. Never pay anyone who promises to speed up your payment. If you are struggling with rent or food now, speak to your college's student support office. Parents and landlords, a little patience this week can keep a student on track.

**[STORY 3]**
Minister Sindisiwe Chikunga, the Minister in the Presidency for Women, Youth and Persons with Disabilities, spoke at a Youth Employment Roundtable in Kempton Park last Thursday. She pointed to South Africa's 257 municipalities and 721 state-owned enterprises. She proposed that each state-owned enterprise offer 500 work placements a year. She said that could create more than 350 thousand opportunities a year. She named Eskom and Transnet as key training grounds. Stats SA's latest figures put unemployment at 47.3 percent for people aged 15 to 34. More than 3.7 million people aged 15 to 24 are not in employment, education or training.
Why it matters: young people struggle to get a first job because they lack work experience. Placements in public bodies are one way to break that cycle, but only a proposal for now.
What to do: young people, keep your CV and certified copies of your documents up to date, so you can apply the day a placement opens. Youth workers and community organisations, ask your local municipality what internship and learnership programmes it runs, and share them widely. Namela keeps a free list of verified bursaries, learnerships and funding at opportunities dot namela dot org.

**[STORY 4]**
The South African Human Rights Commission has released its 2026 State of South African Schools report. It assessed 173 public schools across all nine provinces. It found infrastructure decay, overcrowding and classroom shortages in every province. Limpopo and the Eastern Cape were among the hardest hit. Eight provinces reported water and sanitation problems, and eight reported staff shortages. In some provinces, learners with special educational needs wait up to three years to be assessed and placed. The national Department of Basic Education has 60 days to respond. Provincial departments must submit improvement plans by February 2027.
Why it matters: these are the conditions children learn in every day. The Commission wants time-bound plans to fix school buildings, audits of toilets and sanitation, vacant posts filled faster, and stricter consequences for contractors who abandon projects.
What to do: school governing bodies, check your school against these findings and list what needs fixing. Report unsafe toilets, buildings or water problems to your district office in writing, and keep a copy. Parents whose child is waiting for a special needs assessment, ask the school for the referral date in writing, and follow up with the district office.

**[NUMBER TO REMEMBER]**
44 percent. That is how far the number of babies receiving the Child Support Grant has fallen since 2020. Fewer babies on the grant means fewer families getting support at the most important time in a child's life.
